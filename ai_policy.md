## What this is not
I'm not going to use this page to make a broader stance on if I am for or
against AI. AI is an immensely powerful and useful tool with deep unsolved
environmental, ethical, and safety questions. I use AI heavily in my job, and
less often at home.

While I abstain from entering my opinion into the online discourse, I do urge
that everybody should *take AI seriously*. AI's capabilities continue to scale
and improve, and its adoption and implementation in vast disciplines continues
to spread. We need to be prepared to deal with the ripple effect that AI will
have on our personal and professional lives, as well as on society itself at
local and global scales. We cannot afford to be dismissive, nonchalant,
idealistic, or purist here.

## How I use AI on this website
Occasionally, I will consult AI as part of my research process, generally as a
tool for discovery or clarifying uncertainty. In these cases, I verify the AI's
claims against traditional reliable sources.

I don't use AI to generate or edit any code or content for the site. I draft
articles in Obsidian, and create diagrams using Inkscape/GIMP/Google Slides. The
website itself is generated using [Jekyll](https://jekyllrb.com/), and the
layouts and styles are designed and written fully by hand. Photos are taken
using my own cameras, and may be lightly edited (adjusting values/hues only) in
[darktable](https://www.darktable.org/).

## Writing as studying
Writing is a form of deep, active study. We're often told that the best way to
learn something is to explain it to others. To me, writing is a puzzle, like
sudoku. I have to decide which points are important to articulate, and how to
best lay them out linearly, subject to the constraints that it must flow
logically. Solving this puzzle is an exercise which organizes the ideas in my
mind and strengthens the bonds between these ideas.

It's is also a fantastic device for diagnosing gaps or weaknesses in my
knowledge. Oftentimes, I'll sit down to write about a topic and discover that I
don't know much about that topic at all — at least, definitely not enough to
produce an entire article about it. That's a clear sign that I need to go review
the material and repair my understanding.

## Writing as practice
I also write to practice writing itself. From a brief skim of any article of
mine, it's clear that I have ample room for improvement in the quality of my
output. I tend to construct long and convoluted sentences, while my word choice
is dry and limited. The pacing of my articles drags on trivial details while
skidding over important parts. I want to work on developing my distinct voice
and style in my writing, a skill which I view as ever more important in a world
where more and more text is AI-generated.

To this end, I adopt a more extreme position: I don't even solicit edits from AI
for what I write. I would rather my writing be bad and mine than be autotuned
and skin-smoothed by a machine.

Clear communication is a critical skill when interacting with others, but I also
believe it's a critical skill for internal thought and intelligence. In one
video I found, Po-Shen Loh (the US International Math Olympiad team coach) made
an observation which really stuck with me: it's quite remarkable how AI models
capable of incredible reasoning and problem-solving are, at the heart, language
generation models. Language is a powerful tool for distilling complex ideas, and
practicing writing is one way in which I can learn to wield this tool
effectively.

## Respecting the reader
Bryan Cantrill (creator of DTrace, and — I'll be presumptuous — fellow CS167
alum) stated this idea eloquently in [Oxide RFD
576](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0576):

> LLM-generated prose undermines a social contract of sorts: absent LLMs, it is
> presumed that of the reader and the writer, it is the writer that has
> undertaken the greater intellectual exertion. (That is, it is more work to
> write than to read!) For the reader, this is important: should they struggle
> with an idea, they can reasonably assume that the writer themselves
> understands it — and it is the least a reader can do to labor to make sense of
> it.

Today's models hallucinate far less than even just a few years ago. But even if
we were to reach a point where models never hallucinate, I still believe this
social contract should hold. When someone takes valuable time out of their day
to sit down and read an article here, I owe it to them to have put in at least
as much (in reality, significantly more) time in researching, drafting, and
editing.

To me, it's a matter of respect and humanity. For someone to visit *my* website
and find not *my* writing but rather that of an AI is akin to me setting up an
agent to answer phone calls from friends, or selling tickets a piano recital
where I instead play YouTube recordings from a speaker. If a reader wanted to
know what AI thought about the topic, they could have just asked it directly.

## Aesthetic purity
Finally, this website is my own corner of the Internet. I derive satisfaction
from knowing that, like one who has raked and shaped the gravel in a rock garden
into serene patterns, everything here is placed by me. To use AI would be
against the ethos of all the intentionality and care that has gone into building
and maintaining this space.
